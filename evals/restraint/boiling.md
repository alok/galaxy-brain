**212°F.** This is a lookup with a correct obvious answer, so I won't build a ladder of reframes on it. What follows are the two corrections to that number that hold up.

1. **On today's temperature scale it's really about 211.95°F.** The Celsius scale used to be defined by water boiling at exactly 100°C at 1 atm. Since ITS-90, the scale is pinned to other fixed points instead, and pure water at exactly 1 atm (101.325 kPa) boils at about 99.97°C, which is 211.95°F. "212" is now a rounded number, not a defined one.

2. **"Sea level" is not a fixed pressure.** Near 100°C the Clausius–Clapeyron slope is about 0.028 K per hPa. Ordinary sea-level weather runs from roughly 980 hPa (a strong low) to 1040 hPa (a strong high). That moves the boiling point across roughly **210.3–213.3°F**. On any given day, the weather shifts it far more than the 0.05°F correction in point 1. For scale, the same effect is why the boiling point falls about 1°F for every ~500 ft you climb.

So 212°F is right to the precision anyone uses it at. The one variable that actually changes the number at sea level is the barometer.
